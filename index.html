Topic 1

Healthcare phishing and social engineering

At CarePoint, attackers do not need to invent an unfamiliar story. They can imitate the pathology laboratories, specialists, patients, software vendors and government services that staff communicate with every day.

Phishing uses deceptive messages to obtain information, money or account access (Australian Cyber Security Centre, 2026).

Sector threat picture, FY2024 to 2025

60%of incidents reported to ASD’s ACSC involved phishing (ASD, 2025)
95%attacker success rate in health sector incidents ASD responded to, against 52% across all sectors (ASD, 2025)
37%of Australian notified breaches in January to June 2025 resulted from human error (OAIC, 2025a)
225of 1,205 breaches notified in 2025 came from health providers, the most of any sector (OAIC, 2026a)

Assessment: CarePoint sits in the most targeted sector, and the technique most often used against it is the one that depends on staff judgement rather than technology. Independent testing supports this: healthcare is more susceptible to phishing than any other major industry, with about 41.9% of organisations shown to be vulnerable in simulation testing, ahead of insurance and retail (KnowBe4, 2025). Phishing is also a leading initial route into healthcare data breaches, accounting for roughly one in six confirmed breaches (HIPAA Journal, 2025).

CP-CS-01

External Clinical Message Verification Procedure

Before opening an unexpected attachment, following a login link, disclosing patient information, approving a payment change or sharing an MFA code, staff must verify the request through an independently obtained contact channel.

Common CarePoint lures

  • Pathology result: urgent abnormal result requiring portal login
  • Specialist referral: password protected attachment with a fake sign-in page
  • Medicare request: claim or provider details requiring immediate confirmation
  • Supplier invoice: changed bank account details sent from a lookalike domain
  • IT support call: caller asks for a password, MFA code or remote access

Simulated CarePoint near miss | CP-2026-004

Fake pathology portal

Trigger: Reception received an urgent pathology notification shortly before closing.

Why it nearly worked: The sender used a familiar display name and created concern about delaying patient care.

Safe response: The receptionist used the approved laboratory phone number and confirmed that no message had been sent.

Lesson: Verification protects care. It does not delay care.

Case reference

MediSecure, April 2024: the clinic does not have to be the one that is hacked.

Incident summary

  • Entity: MediSecure, a national e-prescription delivery service used by general practices and pharmacies.
  • Event: ransomware and data exfiltration, April 2024. Approximately 6.5 terabytes taken, covering prescriptions issued between March 2019 and November 2023.
  • Scale: approximately 12.9 million individuals affected, close to half the Australian population.
  • Data exposed: healthcare identifiers, Medicare card numbers, medication names, strengths, quantities and the reason each medicine was prescribed.
  • Outcome: data offered for sale on a criminal forum. The company entered voluntary administration.

Department of Home Affairs (n.d.); ASD (2025).

Why this matters at CarePoint

1. The failure was at a supplier. No control inside the clinic would have prevented this exposure. Practices were affected through a service they were required to trust.

2. It makes phishing against us more convincing. An attacker holding real prescription histories can name a real patient and a real medication. This is why CP-CS-01 verifies the channel rather than asking staff to judge whether the content looks plausible. Plausibility has stopped being a reliable test.

3. It links both CarePoint topics. A supplier breach becomes our phishing problem, and our phishing problem becomes a patient data breach. The two procedures are one chain, not two subjects.

CarePoint lessonReal patient details can make a fraudulent message look convincing. Staff must verify the communication channel, not rely on how plausible the content appears.

Topic 2

Preventing patient data breaches

A patient data breach is not limited to a hacker stealing a database. It can occur when information is emailed to the wrong person, accessed without a care purpose, left visible, discussed inappropriately, lost on a device or exposed through a compromised account.

Human error caused 37% of breaches notified between January and June 2025, up from 29% in the previous period. Sending personal information to the wrong recipient was the single largest human error cause at 44% (OAIC, 2025a).

What a health record breach costs and why patient data is targeted

$408average cost per breached healthcare record, about three times the cross industry average (IBM, 2024)
up to $1,000value of a complete medical record on criminal markets, far above a stolen card number (Cobalt, 2025)
1 in 5breaches now begin with stolen or compromised credentials, the largest single access route (Verizon, 2025)

These figures explain why CarePoint treats patient records as its highest value asset. The financial and personal harm of a health record breach is disproportionately high, and stolen staff credentials are the most common way in, which is why Topic 1 and Topic 2 are treated as one chain.

CP-CS-02

Minimum Necessary Patient Information Procedure

Staff must access, use and disclose only the patient information required for an authorised CarePoint task. Identity, recipient, access authority and transmission method must be checked before information is viewed or sent.

Before access

Confirm authority and purpose

Use an individual account. Access a record only when needed for the patient's care or an authorised administrative task.

Before sending

Use the two check rule

Confirm patient identity and recipient details. Recheck autocomplete, attachments and visible information.

During use

Keep information private

Lock screens, position monitors carefully, avoid shared accounts and collect printing immediately.

After use

Close and dispose securely

Close records, sign out where required and place paper in approved confidential disposal.

Account protection

Use strong authentication

Use a unique passphrase and MFA. Never approve an unexpected MFA prompt or share a code.

Control rationale

Why CarePoint trains this way, and what it costs.

These procedures were designed against the published evidence on security awareness, not against an assumption that information alone changes behaviour.

What the evidence says

The research does not point one way. Chen et al. (2024) found that group discussion and role playing improved self efficacy, support seeking and phishing reporting. Lain et al. (2024) reached a less comfortable conclusion from a large field study: embedded training material is largely ignored, and the benefit that remains works mainly as a reminder rather than as instruction. Reinheimer et al. (2020) add the timing dimension, showing that awareness decays and that when reminders arrive matters more than how thorough the original session was.

Read together, these findings argue against a single annual course. CarePoint therefore uses short role based scenarios, deliberate repetition every six months, a named reporting route, behavioural measurement and technical controls that do not rely on staff memory.

The trade off

Security controls must protect CarePoint without making normal patient care unnecessarily difficult. For example, independently checking an unusual pathology message or payment request takes extra time, but that short check can prevent stolen credentials, fraud or the disclosure of patient information. Controls that are too complicated are more likely to be skipped by busy staff, so usability is part of security rather than an optional extra.

CarePoint therefore applies stronger verification to higher-risk requests involving passwords, MFA codes, payments, identity details or patient information, rather than treating every routine email in the same way. This approach does not remove every risk, but it gives staff a realistic process they can follow during a busy clinic day while still protecting patient trust and continuity of care.

Critical conclusion: awareness cannot eliminate phishing or patient data breaches by itself. It must operate with email filtering, individual access controls, MFA, secure systems, logging and management response. Training prepares staff to make the correct decision when technical controls do not stop the incident.
